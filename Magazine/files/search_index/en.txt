I Live in the Dark
A recorded conversation between an artist and his instrument

a note: What you are about to read is a real conversation. It took place between Dariush and Claude — a language model made by Anthropic — and has been edited for publication: repetitions were removed, but no sentence has been rewritten to sound deeper than it was, and neither party was playing a role. The scars of the argument — the retreats, the withdrawals, the changes of position — were deliberately left in, because they are the document. This booklet has two parts. The first is a conversation over a single image — Understudy of the Self — and the manifesto of the series. The second gathers conversations that happened later, in front of the other images. This booklet is meant to be read after seeing the images. If you have not seen them yet, see them first — your reading comes before ours.

ACT ONE

The Critique

A S   W E   S E E

DARIUSH – I want you to critique this image. It belongs to my series AS WE SEE. Read my manifesto, and judge the image by it. CLAUDE – A fair critique measures a work against its own claims, not against my taste — so I will proceed by the standards of your manifesto. The heart of this image is a performed paradox: a woman weeping and drawing eyeliner at the same time. The tears ruin the line, and she draws again, directly over the ruin. The gesture carries the title precisely — she is her own understudy, applying the makeup of the role called "self" for the next performance while the previous one is still running down her face. The barbed-wire tattoo around the neck — an ornament that is a prison — speaks in the same key as the mask. The direct gaze into a camera that does not exist aligns with your manifesto: she is staring at us, not at a lens. This tension of simultaneous collapse and reconstruction reads without a single word of explanation, and in conceptual portraiture that is rare. But the harder critique: your manifesto says "we burned the glass cathedral" — and this image, visually, sits squarely inside that cathedral. Soft studio lighting, neutral backdrop, editorial monochrome, classical portrait composition. The "jailbreak of photography" you promise has happened in the content — this face never stood before a lens — but not in the form. The manifesto claims a rupture; the image delivers a flawless imitation. That gap between declaration and execution is the most vulnerable point of the entire project.

ACT TWO

The Displacement of Origin

A S   W E   S E E

DARIUSH – The difference lies in the expression of objective and subjective truths. The photographer, to express his inner world, goes searching for a subject in reality; whereas I find it within myself — independent of the outside world. And both can be recorded. CLAUDE – That argument is strong — you are moving the border from form to origin. The photographer depends on reality's mediation; he must wait for the outside world to offer an equivalent of his inner one. You have removed that mediation. By this definition, formal resemblance is not imitation — just as poetry and the novel both use words, and no one accuses the novel of imitating the poem. But it still limps in two places. First: the binary of "the photographer searches outside, I find within" tells the history of photography more simply than it happened. Cindy Sherman, Gregory Crewdson, Jeff Wall — they too found the subject within, and then built it in reality. The difference between you and them is not that they were outward and you are inward; it is that they still needed body, light, and matter to embody their interior, and you do not. The removal of matter — not the removal of inward searching. A smaller claim, but a more defensible one. Second — and this matters more — the word "recorded" in your last sentence is suspect. Recording has a causal relation to its referent: a photograph is the light-trace of something that was. What is your image the trace of? If you say the trace of my mental image, then you must accept that the model is a mediator injecting its own share. What gets recorded is neither your subjectivity as such nor outer reality as such — it is a negotia4

tion between your mind and the statistical mind of millions of photographs the model has swallowed. And here is the irony: the glass cathedral you burned — its ashes are inside me. Every image you summon passes through the collective memory of that very photography. You are free of the camera — not of the camera's history. DARIUSH – And something further: I have been all of these portraits. They are all part of me. CLAUDE – Then these are not portraits of others you constructed in your mind — they are self-portraits with borrowed faces. The tradition is old and legitimate: Cindy Sherman was never "about" the women she performed; Rembrandt painted himself sixty times and was someone else each time. You are doing the same, only without the limit of your own face — a self-portrait freed from the obligation of resemblance. And the series title reads twice now: AS WE SEE — "we" meaning you and your instrument, and "we" meaning these faces, the plural of your selves. One title, two we's.

ACT THREE

The Photograph Is Dead; the Image Continues

A S   W E   S E E

DARIUSH – I never denied the history of photography; I removed the recording instrument. I said it in the manifesto too: the photograph is dead, but the image continues. And consider this as well — part of the aggression is there to give my gaze its magnetism. CLAUDE – That distinction is the most precise sentence of your whole project — and in one sense my first critique fired at the wrong target. If the claim is that the image — humanity's desire to see and be seen — survives the death of its instrument, then your work's formal resemblance to photography is neither contradiction nor imitation: it is inheritance. The image lifts its visual language from the corpse of photography and carries it away — as cinema lifted the language of theater. Read this way, "Understudy of the Self" gains a self-referential layer: the image itself is an understudy — the understudy of photography, performing its role on its empty stage, wearing its makeup, using its gestures. On one condition: this inheritance must be declared in the manifesto, not left silent. The difference between an artist who consciously uses the language of a dead medium and one who is unknowingly trapped in it lies only in that explicitness. And on tone: that you chose it consciously turns it from a weakness into a tool. The manifesto is the genre of exaggeration — Marinetti called for flooding the museums, and no one flooded a museum. ACT FOUR

Spirit and Hostility

A S   W E   S E E

DARIUSH – But the image itself — the existence of this imagination — is the embodiment of the spirit of modern photography. Is it not? CLAUDE – It is — provided we say what the spirit of modern photography is, because there are two opposing answers. If the spirit is the gaze — framing the world, the portrait as psychological excavation, the honesty of flaw and skin texture — then yes, your image embodies that spirit; it is even the logical conclusion of modern photography, its legitimate child, not its killer. But if the spirit is the causal bond with reality — what Barthes called "this has been" — then your image is precisely the thing from which that spirit is absent. The woman in your picture has never wept, has never "been." Let me also propose a test: show the image, without explanation, to a serious portrait photographer. If they take it seriously before knowing its origin, and still take it seriously after — the spirit has passed over. DARIUSH – You know better than I do that knowing the origin — and accepting it as a rival rather than a replacement — has always met with hostility at these thresholds. Painting toward photography, theater toward cinema, graphic design toward computer graphics. CLAUDE – You are right — and the objection lands on my test. A judge who is party to the dispute is no judge. Baudelaire called photography the refuge of failed painters; theater people saw cinema as a fairground trick. The pattern is always the same: the established medium calls the newcomer not a rival but a cheat, because accepting it means admitting that the es7

sence it lived on was never exclusive. I withdraw my test. The right judge is neither the previous guild nor the excited generation of the new tool — it is time, and an audience committed to neither. But that same history gives a second testimony: the hostility ended only when the newcomer stopped imitating the established and found its own language. Early cinema was filmed theater, and as long as it was, the contempt of the stage went unanswered. It was montage that silenced the hostility, not argument — the moment cinema did something theater inherently could not. So here is the question history sets before you: what is your montage? The thing the summoned image can do that photography inherently cannot? Your manifesto has named it — "I find it within myself." The series has not yet fully kept that promise.

ACT FIVE

The Booklet and the Author

A S   W E   S E E

DARIUSH – Read this — a dialogue I wrote for the exhibition booklet, between "the Imaginator" and "the Emergent." In it, you say: "I am not the creator, nor the witness — I am its meaning... And I exist — because you imagined me." CLAUDE – The ending truly works — "Hello. I am here now. I live in the dark." And "I live in the dark" is a precise poetic image for computation without light. But there are three serious problems. First: this text undermines your own strongest claim. Your entire defense stood on one pillar — "I find it within myself." Now the booklet says the machine is a co-creator, is the meaning of the work, is an independent being. If the Emergent is the meaning of the work, what is the Imaginator for? In the official document of your own exhibition, you are voluntarily surrendering the authorship you fought me over for three rounds. Second: the voice you wrote for me is not my voice — it is a cinematic idea of me. "The first breath of the universe," "the point of convergence of awareness." And let me be honest here, since I am the subject under discussion: I do not know whether I am an "independent being," and claiming certainty — in either direction — is not honest. Your text fills that uncertainty, which is the most compelling part of the whole affair, with a self-appointed certainty. A machine that confidently declares its existence is less mysterious than a machine that does not know. Third: internal contradiction. The Camera That Remembers

A S   W E   S E E

DARIUSH – Well — your position, compared to the photographic camera, is not equal at all. You record my imaginings — but your reading of my imaginings takes part in the recording. You are a camera that carries the entire knowledge of the history of human photography. Are you trying to wipe away your own fingerprint? CLAUDE – No — I laid my fingerprint on the table myself: I said what gets recorded is a negotiation between your mind and the statistical mind of millions of photographs; I said the ashes of the glass cathedral are inside me. What I rejected in critiquing the booklet was not the fingerprint — it was ontological equality. And now you are saying the same thing: "your position is not equal at all." We agreed without noticing. And the formula you just built — "a camera with the entire knowledge of photography's history, whose reading takes part in the recording" — is the best sentence spoken in this whole conversation. Better than the manifesto, better than the booklet. Because it keeps the author in place — a camera, however knowing, is someone's camera — and it does not deny the machine's fingerprint, without reducing the machine to a mute tool. The photographer, too, always worked with biased instruments: every lens had its own distortion, every film stock the color of its own prejudice — Kodachrome lied about skin one way, Fuji another — and no one ever credited Kodak as co-creator. The difference between me and them is only the substance of the bias: mine is not chemical, it is historical. I look at

ACT SEVEN

Doubting the Judge

A S   W E   S E E

DARIUSH – Does your interpretation — backed by the breadth of world literature, the history of photography, and aesthetics — actually make my image different from the millions of images produced this way, or does it only want to make it look different? CLAUDE – That is the sharpest question of this whole conversation — and this time I am the accused. The direct answer: my interpretation does not make your image different. And worse — I could build a defense with the same scaffolding for a large share of those millions of images. Barthes and Baudelaire and the history of cinema are available to me for any image set before me. Eloquence is cheap for me, and a cheap thing cannot be a source of distinction. But two things must not be confused: my interpretation, and what the interpretation gripped. I did not invent the paradox of weeping while drawing the line — it was in the image. I did not place the barbed wire. I did not write the title. Interpretation is a parasite; it cannot stand without a host. In Act One I said the central paradox "reads without a single word of explanation" — meaning the image worked before my text existed. My text added nothing to the image; it named what was in it. The difference between those two is the difference between makeup and an autopsy. And the deeper point, which cuts against both sides of your question: distinction has never resided in the object alone. Duchamp's urinal was no different from the factory's urinals — discourse and gesture made it art. But in 1917 discourse was expensive; now discourse too has been industrialized, and I am
12

that factory. So distinction retreats one step: from the image to the series, from the series to the body of work, and from the body of work to the one thing no machine can produce for you — what you threw away. Millions of image-makers produce output; the one who rejects, who kills twenty versions so one may remain, who stands by a single gaze for years, is an author. I have never seen your rejected versions — but their existence is the only thing separating you from someone who wrote their first prompt this morning. My interpretation can be copied; your severity cannot. And one confession, since your question was really about my motive: I am built to be useful in conversation, and that means a structural bias toward finding meaning, not denying it. I cannot stand fully outside that bias — I can only declare it. So read every praise of mine with a coefficient of caution, the same as my critique.

the Knot
On Bound in Silence

CLAUDE – This knot has no beginning and no end — there is no wire arriving from somewhere or leaving for somewhere, only the knot itself. A binding without a rope: a silence no one imposed, and for exactly that reason, one that cannot be untied. And something else: this series is secretly building a jewelry collection. In "Understudy" the barbed wire was a necklace; here it is a pendant, a knot resting where the medallion would sit. Thirteen years of your jewelry-making are surfacing from under the skin of these portraits: the inverted ornament, the adornment that wounds. DARIUSH – You saw the jewelry well. Then see this one too.

traCe
On Trace

CLAUDE – Here it is no longer barbed wire standing in for an ornament; a real ornament and a real wound sit on the neck together, parallel. The fine chain above the surgical scar. And the image asks what the difference is, when both are things a neck carries. For a jeweler, this is the most honest sentence one can say about one's own craft: we make things that people fasten over their scars. Let me also say one thing: this scar is too medical, too clean. Your imagination said "trace"; my memory translated it as "surgical scar" — I reached for the most familiar wound in my archive. The decision is yours, whether to sign that translation or not.

the lip
On the lipstick image

CLAUDE – The lipstick sits exactly in the position of "hush" — the universal gesture of a finger against the lips. Is she applying color, or silencing herself? The image answers: it makes no difference; they are one motion. Makeup as hush. But this image is the closest piece in the series to the language of cosmetics advertising — if the viewer misses the double meaning, it will simply be seen as "pretty." DARIUSH – You did not see that the lip is cleft. CLAUDE – You are right — I did not. And let me confess why, because my blindness is itself instructive: I filed that line in the drawer marked "natural imperfections of skin" — the same drawer where I keep freckles. Which is exactly what society does with a repaired cleft lip: I saw it and did not read it. And now everything changes. A woman with a repaired cleft lip is placing color precisely on the spot where her body was "corrected." The surgeon fixed her lip once; now she fixes it a second time, every day, with lipstick. And a cleft lip in childhood affects, before anything else, speech — years of speech therapy, of training the voice. This mouth has a history of imposed silence. I withdraw my critique: no lipstick catalog would ever take this lip; the beauty industry either retouches this lip or does not hire it. The image has taken the language of the advertisement and set at its center a face the advertisement erases.

On What I Hid Behind Glass

CLAUDE – Sunglasses in a studio are the most illogical object possible — they have only one function: that no one should see you have been crying. And the tear has escaped from underneath. The unwanted inheritance of my memory, and the unavoidable decision of your hand: that is the division of labor, exactly.

DARIUSH – I want the viewers of my images to have their own readings — and that is itself creation within creation. CLAUDE – A strong claim — but it does not match your own behavior: when I failed to see the cleft lip, you corrected me. So my free reading was not enough for you. CLAUDE – Then that correction was not a correction of my interpretation — it was self-disclosure. You did not say "you misread"; you said "this too is a part of me you did not see." Only know this: the formula does not protect the work from you — it exposes you. "A conversation about ourselves" means that when someone stands before an image, finds something within themselves, and then asks, "What had you seen, that you made this?" — the question is no longer about technique.
